# BTD — Below / Table Stakes / Differentiate

> A framework for making intentional positioning decisions on any product dimension.

**Origin:** Shreyas Doshi — [20 Product with Harry Stebbings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TsIgLxr95pQ)

---

## The core idea

For any feature, capability, or product area, you have three intentional choices:

| Level | What it means | When to choose it |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| **B — Below table stakes** | You don't meet user expectations. You accept the gap consciously. | When the gap doesn't affect your target segment's core job |
| **T — Table stakes** | You meet expectations. Users don't complain, but it's not a reason to choose you. | When the dimension matters but isn't where you win |
| **D — Differentiate** | You go significantly above expectations for a specific segment. Your competitive edge. | When this is the core job of an underserved segment |

---

## Vocabulary

**Table stakes** comes from poker — the minimum chips needed to sit at the table. Not impressive to have. Disqualifying not to have.

In product: what users expect as a baseline just to consider you seriously.

| English | French |
|---------|--------|
| Table stakes | Le minimum requis / "être dans la course" |
| Below table stakes | En dessous du seuil d'acceptabilité |
| Differentiate | Se différencier / créer un avantage distinctif |

Note: in French product circles, "table stakes" is often kept in English, or replaced with **"facteur d'hygiène"** (from Herzberg) — same logic: doesn't motivate if present, demotivates if absent.

**Mental test:**
- T = users won't notice it's there
- B = users will notice it's missing
- D = users will switch *because* it's there

---

## Why it matters

The default PM instinct is to reach T on everything — close all gaps, keep all users happy. Against an incumbent, this is a trap: you spend years catching up and you're still behind, because they kept moving too.

BTD forces a different question: **who are you building for, and what does their job require you to be great at?**

If you've nailed the JTBD for a specific segment, you know what D should be. Everything else is T or B — consciously accepted, not forgotten.

---

## The key discipline

BTD choices must be **explicit and intentional**. The failure mode isn't choosing B — it's never deciding. When you haven't decided, everything drifts to vague T-minus and you win on nothing.

Questions to ask in any roadmap or prioritization conversation:
- *"Are we choosing to differentiate here, or accepting table stakes?"*
- *"If we're choosing B — are we comfortable with that gap for our target segment?"*
- *"What does our target segment's core job require us to be great at?"*

---

## In practice

**Example:** New B2B invoicing tool competing against Sage.
- Sage covers everything adequately. Winning by covering everything slightly better is not a strategy.
- Target segment: freelancers who live in Gmail. Core job: send an invoice and get paid without leaving their workflow.
- **D** on Gmail integration and payment tracking.
- **B** on multi-currency, VAT complexity, advanced accounting exports.
- That segment tolerates the B gaps because the D move solves their actual job completely.

---

## Common traps

- **Accidental B** — you never decided, you just deprioritized. The gap exists but no one owns it.
- **T as ambition** — the team celebrates hitting table stakes as a win. No one switched because of it.
- **D spread too thin** — trying to differentiate on 5 dimensions for 3 segments simultaneously. D requires focus.
- **Ignoring who B affects** — a gap that's fine for your target segment may disqualify you with another. Know who you're serving before accepting B.
