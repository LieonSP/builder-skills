# Retro-Planning — Short-Timeline Delivery Method

Source: applied on the O'clock training mission (Gerard) — 3 weeks of content to prepare, mission already 1 week in when the constraint became visible. Built in ~10 minutes on a table in a restaurant. Immediately useful — a pattern to reproduce, not a one-off.

---

## The core idea

When a deliverable has a hard external deadline and a fixed, non-negotiable set of personal constraints (days off, travel, family time), don't plan forward from today — plan backward from the deadline, slot the constraints in first, and only then see how much room is left for the work itself.

This is the same backward-planning logic as `document-preparation.md`, but generalized beyond documents: it applies to any short-timeline project where content/deliverables must exist by a date, and where the real risk isn't "will I finish" but "will I still be optimizing when I should already be done."

---

## When to use it

- Timeline is short (days to a few weeks), not months
- There's a real personal constraint competing for the same time (family travel, days off, another commitment) that must not be sacrificed
- The known failure mode is **optimization obsession** — the temptation to keep polishing content past the point of diminishing returns, until there's no runway left before a non-movable event (departure, presentation, delivery)

---

## The method

1. **Anchor on the real deadline** — not the mission end date, but the last moment content/work can still be adjusted (e.g., day before departure, not day of delivery).
2. **Block the non-negotiables first** — mark every day off, travel day, family commitment on the calendar *before* placing any work. These are fixed costs, not slack to be reclaimed later.
3. **Work backward in units of content/deliverable**, not in days — for each unit (e.g., a week of course content, a chapter, a module), assign a target completion date counting back from the deadline.
4. **Name the ship criteria per unit** — "good enough to deliver" for each piece, so the plan enforces a stopping point instead of leaving it to in-the-moment judgment (which is exactly where optimization obsession takes over).
5. **Keep it visible and simple** — a table with dates is enough. The value is the forcing function, not the artifact's polish. This one took 10 minutes on a table in a restaurant.

---

## Why this works

- **Makes the tradeoff visible before it's a crisis** — instead of discovering at day 18 of 21 that there's no time left, the constraint is priced in from day 1
- **Converts a vague anxiety ("I need to balance this") into a concrete date** — "unit 3 must be done by [date]" is actionable; "I should pace myself" is not
- **Defends the non-negotiables structurally** — days off aren't something to renegotiate under pressure once the plan already assumes them
- **Interrupts the 90%→95% pattern** — a target date per unit forces a decision to move on, rather than letting flow-state momentum decide

---

## PM trap to avoid

Building the retro-plan once and never checking it again. The plan is only useful if actual progress is compared against the target dates regularly — otherwise it's just a document that felt good to make, which is itself a mild form of the optimization trap (see: "Watch for over-optimization" in the main Eagle CLAUDE.md).
