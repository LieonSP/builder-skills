# Switching Interviews

> A qualitative research method for uncovering the real reason customers switch — to or away from your product.
> Developed by **Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek** (The Re-Wired Group), grounded in JTBD theory.

---

## What it is

A switching interview is a structured conversation with someone who has recently made a buying decision — either switching *to* your product, switching *away*, or switching from a competitor. The goal is not to collect opinions or satisfaction scores. It's to **reconstruct the causal story** of why someone changed their behavior.

The core diagnostic question:
> "What was happening in your world when you first started looking for an alternative?"

This surfaces the **struggling moment** — the trigger that set the switch in motion. Everything before and after that moment is context that explains the job.

---

## Why it works better than standard churn interviews

Standard churn interviews ask: "What did you dislike?" → produces feature complaints.

Switching interviews ask: "Walk me through what was happening in your life before, during, and after this decision." → produces **job context**.

Customers cannot reliably predict or explain their future behavior. But they can narrate past events accurately — especially the emotional texture of the decision. The past-tense, story-based framing bypasses rationalization and surfaces real causality.

---

## The Four Forces Model

Every switching decision is shaped by four forces acting simultaneously. The switch happens when the top two outweigh the bottom two.

```
[ABOVE THE LINE — forces driving change]
  Push of the current situation   →  what's broken, frustrating, or insufficient right now
  Pull of the new solution        →  the appeal of the new product or approach

[BELOW THE LINE — forces resisting change]
  Anxiety about the new          →  fear that the new solution won't work, or that you'll fail with it
  Habit of the present           →  the inertia of what you're already doing and know how to do
```

**Key insight for product teams:** Most teams default to increasing *pull* (adding features). But often the switch is blocked by *anxiety* — which you can reduce by removing friction, adding reassurance, or simplifying onboarding. The answer is sometimes to take things out, not add more.

---

## The Switching Timeline

Purchases follow a consistent arc. The interview should walk through each phase:

```
First Thought → Passive Looking → [Triggering Event] → Active Looking → Decision → Purchase → Consumption
```

- **First thought**: the earliest moment the person sensed something wasn't working. Often weeks or months before any action.
- **Passive looking**: they're aware of the problem but not yet investing time — "if something better comes along, I'll notice."
- **Triggering event**: something concrete that tips them from passive to active. This is the struggling moment. Find it.
- **Active looking**: they're now evaluating alternatives seriously. What did they search for? Who did they talk to? What almost won?
- **Decision**: the specific moment they committed. What tipped it?
- **Consumption**: what happened after? Were expectations met? This reveals whether the job was actually done.

## How to run a switching interview

**Who to recruit:**
- Recent switchers — people who made the decision in the last 30–90 days (memory is still accurate)
- Churned customers who left for a competitor (not those who simply stopped)
- New customers who came from a direct competitor

**Session format:**
- 45–60 minutes, one-on-one
- No survey or questionnaire — narrative only
- Record (with consent) so you can re-listen for nuance

**Opening frame:**
> "I'm not looking for opinions or feedback on our product. I just want to understand the story of how you ended up making this decision. No right or wrong answers — just your experience."

**Core question sequence:**
1. "What did you [buy / switch to]? When did that happen?"
2. "When did you first have the thought that something needed to change?"
3. "What was going on in your life at that time?"
4. "What did you try before making the switch?"
5. "Was there a moment where you said 'okay, I need to actually do something about this'? What happened?"
6. "How did [new product] come onto your radar?"
7. "What almost stopped you from switching?"
8. "Now that you've been using it — does it feel like it's doing what you needed?"

**Probing for the struggling moment:**
- "Tell me more about that situation."
- "What was that like?"
- "What were you thinking when that happened?"
- "What did you do next?"

## What to look for across interviews

After 5–8 interviews, patterns emerge. Look for:

- **The triggering event** — is there a consistent struggle that precipitates the search?
- **The job being hired for** — what progress was the customer trying to make? Not "what feature did they want" but "what outcome were they building toward?"
- **The anxieties that almost blocked the switch** — what nearly stopped them? This is where onboarding and trust-building interventions live.
- **The competitors in the customer's mind** — often not who you think. The real competition might be a spreadsheet, a workaround, or doing nothing.
- **The moment of commitment** — what made the tipping point? A specific feature, a conversation, a pricing trigger?

## When to use switching interviews

- You're seeing churn you don't understand — especially when usage metrics look healthy right before cancellation
- You're entering a new market segment and don't know why customers would switch to you
- Win rates are declining in a specific segment
- You want to identify the job before building, not after
- You're repositioning an existing product and need to understand what job it's currently being hired for

**Skip when:** You already have strong causal evidence from behavioral data and the question is execution, not diagnosis.

---

## Common traps

**Usage data as a proxy for job success.** High feature usage before churn means nothing about whether the job was done. A customer who used your inbox heavily every day might have been struggling, not succeeding.

**Jumping from job → solution.** The switching interview tells you the job. It does not generate the solution. Stay in the problem space longer than feels comfortable before proposing features.

**Asking about hypothetical future behavior.** "What would make you stay?" is a trap. People cannot predict their behavior. Stick to the past.

**Recruiting happy customers.** Satisfied customers will tell you what they like. Churned or switched customers will tell you what actually caused change — which is more valuable for innovation.
